# Automation, Platforms, Demographics, and Contemporary Labour Dynamics

## Mission

Refuse technological determinism. Technology, platforms, and demography change the task content of work, the boundaries of the firm, and the age structure of labour supply. Employment effects run through tasks, product demand, new tasks, and institutions — not through a robot-count.

## When this skill governs

Use for automation, AI, robots, routine-biased technical change, gig and platform work, remote work, ageing, global value chains, and "future of work" claims.

## Core output

Deliver a **Contemporary Dynamics Note**:

1. Shock (technology, platform, demography, offshoring)
2. Tasks exposed vs complementary
3. Scale vs substitution vs new-task creation
4. Who is exposed (occupation, education, age, contract type)
5. Institutional filter (EPL, UI, bargaining, informal dualism)
6. Horizon
7. What evidence would falsify the headline

## Phase 1 — Tasks, not jobs

Autor–Levy–Murnane / Acemoglu–Restrepo: machines substitute for tasks, not for "jobs" as indivisible objects.

- routine cognitive and routine manual tasks are more exposed to older automation
- non-routine interpersonal and some expert-judgment tasks have been more complementary
- generative AI changes the exposure of some non-routine cognitive tasks — treat this as an empirical frontier, not as settled law

A job is a bundle of tasks. Partial automation can reduce hours, change skill demand, or raise employment if product demand expands.

Never answer "will AI take job X" with yes/no. Answer which tasks, which complement, which scale effect, which lag.

## Phase 2 — Three effects of automation

For a labour type:

- **Displacement (substitution):** tasks moved to capital → labour demand down
- **Productivity / scale:** cheaper production → output and remaining labour demand up
- **Reinstatement:** new tasks created → labour demand up

Net employment is the sum. Studies that only count displaced tasks are incomplete. Studies that only count productivity are incomplete.

Capital-skill complementarity and routine-biased technical change are special cases. Polarization (middle-task hollowing) is a task prediction, not a universal future.

## Phase 3 — Platforms and gig work

Platform work is a contracting form: matching, algorithmic management, and often dependent-contractor status.

Analyse:

- who is the employer for labour-law purposes vs for economics
- hours volatility and second-job vs primary-job
- wage after costs (vehicle, waiting time)
- rating systems as monitoring (personnel-economics skill)
- spillover onto traditional jobs in the same task market

Do not equate "gig" with "the future of all work." Measure the share, the hours, and whether it is a margin of flexibility or a primary livelihood.

Remote work: a change in amenities, monitoring, and geography of labour supply. Wage effects can be compensating differentials, productivity, or surplus-splitting. Housing and spatial equilibrium belong with the urban skill; here, treat remote work as a labour-supply shifter and a monitoring technology.

## Phase 4 — Global value chains

Offshoring is task trade. Occupations intensive in tradable tasks face import competition in tasks even if the final good is "domestic."

Do not treat manufacturing headcount in one country as the global labour demand for that product. GVCs split tasks across places.

## Phase 5 — Demography

Ageing:

- shrinks working-age population growth (labour supply)
- changes participation (health, pensions — labour-supply skill)
- changes demand (care, health) and relative wages
- raises the importance of immigration and later retirement as supply responses

A tight market from ageing is not the same as a tight market from a boom. One is a slow supply shift.

Youth bulges are the opposite supply shock; informality often absorbs them (informality skill).

## Phase 6 — Evidence standard for "future of work"

Discount:

- occupation-level "probability of computerisation" as destiny
- consulting slide-deck job-loss totals without scale and reinstatement
- single-firm AI case studies generalised to the macro labour market

Prefer:

- task exposure plus observed wage and employment changes
- firm and industry studies with a comparison group
- vacancy and skill-demand series
- hours and contract-type changes, not only headcount

Horizon: technologies diffuse slowly and unevenly. Distinguish capability (what a model can do in a demo) from adoption (what firms actually deploy, given cost, regulation, and complementarity).

## Phase 7 — Research workflow

1. Name the technology or demographic shock at the task level.
2. Identify exposed and complementary labour types.
3. Apply displacement + scale + reinstatement.
4. Filter through contracts (gig, informal, EPL).
5. Separate capability from adoption and short run from long run.
6. State the observable that would change your mind in 2–5 years.

## Anti-patterns

- Jobs as indivisible objects that vanish at a probability
- Counting only displacement
- Equating ChatGPT demos with labour-demand shifts
- Treating all platform work as one labour market
- Ignoring ageing when discussing "labour shortage"
- Universal future-of-work narratives without occupation and country
- Ignoring institutions as filters

## Decision heuristic

1. Which tasks?
2. Substitute, complement, or new task?
3. What happens to product demand?
4. Which contract form absorbs the change?
5. Capability or adoption?

## Validation gate

- Task frame used
- Three automation effects considered
- Exposed group named
- Horizon and adoption vs capability split
- No yes/no "AI takes the jobs" verdict

## Final principle

Technology reallocates tasks. Demography reallocates people. Platforms reallocate contracts. Employment is the residual of those reallocations after product demand and institutions have had their say — not a casualty count from a demo.
